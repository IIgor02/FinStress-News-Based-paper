\section{Methodology}\label{sec:methodology}

This section presents our methodology for constructing the Financial Stress Index (FSI) and analyzing its predictive content for sovereign credit risk. We develop three distinct approaches to FSI construction---dictionary-based, machine learning-based, and news-based---and combine them into a unified index. The predictive relationships are assessed using Local Projections impulse response functions and Granger causality tests.

\subsection{Data Sources and Sample Period}

Our analysis spans February 2008 to November 2025, comprising 214 monthly observations. The sample period encompasses several major financial stress episodes, including the Global Financial Crisis (2008--2009), the Brazilian recession and political crisis (2015--2016), the COVID-19 pandemic (2020), and subsequent fiscal uncertainty episodes (2021--2024).

We collect data from three primary sources. Google Trends provides weekly Search Volume Index (SVI) data for Portuguese-language financial stress queries. The SVI is normalized on a 0--100 scale, where 100 represents peak search interest for a given term within the selected period and geographic region. Brazil 5-Year Credit Default Swap spreads, obtained from Investing.com, serve as our measure of sovereign credit risk. CDS spreads represent the cost of insurance against Brazilian sovereign default, with higher values indicating greater perceived default risk. Daily IBOVESPA data from Yahoo Finance allows us to compute 21-day realized volatility as a measure of equity market stress. All series are aggregated to monthly frequency to reduce noise and ensure alignment across sources.

\subsection{FSI Construction Methods}

We implement three methodologically distinct approaches to constructing the FSI, drawing on established literature in text-based financial indicators. Each approach captures different aspects of financial stress perception.

\subsubsection{Dictionary-Based FSI}

Following \cite{da2011search}, we construct a dictionary-based FSI using pre-specified search queries that capture financial stress in the Brazilian context. Our query set comprises 25 Portuguese terms organized into five tiers reflecting different dimensions of financial stress. The first tier contains direct crisis terminology such as ``crise financeira'' (financial crisis) and ``crash bolsa'' (stock market crash). The second tier captures market stress through terms like ``queda ibovespa'' (IBOVESPA decline) and ``volatilidade bolsa'' (stock market volatility). Subsequent tiers address sovereign risk, currency and banking concerns, and investor sentiment.

The FSI is computed as a weighted average of Search Volume Indices:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fsi_dict}
    \text{FSI}_t^{\text{Dict}} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i \cdot \text{SVI}_{i,t}}{\sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i}
\end{equation}
where $\text{SVI}_{i,t}$ denotes the Search Volume Index for query $i$ at time $t$, and $w_i$ represents the tier weight (1.5 for Tier 1, 1.2 for Tier 2, and 1.0 for remaining tiers). The weighting scheme assigns greater importance to terms that most directly signal financial distress. The resulting index is normalized to a $[0, 1]$ scale using min-max normalization, where 0 indicates minimum observed stress and 1 indicates maximum stress.

\subsubsection{Machine Learning FSI}

Following \cite{garcia2023machine}, we implement a LASSO regression approach that learns which search queries best predict future market declines. Rather than relying on expert judgment to select relevant queries, this data-driven method allows the regularization procedure to identify predictive signals. The model regresses next-period IBOVESPA returns on current-period search volumes:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lasso}
    r_{t+1} = \alpha + \sum_{i=1}^{N} \beta_i \cdot \text{SVI}_{i,t} + \varepsilon_{t+1}
\end{equation}
where $r_{t+1}$ is the IBOVESPA return from $t$ to $t+1$, $\beta_i$ are coefficients subject to LASSO penalization ($\sum|\beta_i| \leq \lambda$), and $\lambda$ is the regularization parameter selected via cross-validation. The ML-FSI is defined as the negative of the predicted return, such that higher values indicate anticipated market declines.

\subsubsection{News-Based FSI}

Following \cite{baker2016measuring}, we construct a news-based FSI using textual analysis of Brazilian financial news articles. This approach captures stress signals from professional financial journalism rather than retail investor search behavior, potentially offering a different perspective on market stress.

We analyze 33,881 financial news articles from three major Brazilian news sources: Folha de S\~ao Paulo, Valor Econ\^omico, and G1. The sample spans June 2002 to December 2025, providing over two decades of news coverage. Articles are classified as stress-related using a two-way co-occurrence method: an article is flagged if it simultaneously contains at least one financial term from a dictionary of 99 Portuguese financial terms and at least one stress or negative term from combined dictionaries of 162 stress terms and 289 negative terms.

For each qualifying article, we compute a weighted stress score:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:news_score}
    S_a = \sqrt{F_a \times (2 \cdot T_a^{\text{stress}} + T_a^{\text{neg}})}
\end{equation}
where $S_a$ is the stress score for article $a$, $F_a$ is the count of financial terms, $T_a^{\text{stress}}$ is the count of stress terms, and $T_a^{\text{neg}}$ is the count of negative terms. Stress terms receive double weight as they directly indicate financial distress. The index is aggregated to weekly frequency and normalized to a $[0, 1]$ scale.

\subsubsection{Combined FSI}

We construct a combined index that synthesizes information from all three approaches. The combination uses inverse-variance weighting:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:combined}
    \text{FSI}_t^{\text{Comb}} = \frac{\sum_{j} \sigma_{j}^{-2} \cdot \text{FSI}_t^{j}}{\sum_{j} \sigma_{j}^{-2}}
\end{equation}
where $j \in \{\text{Dict}, \text{ML}, \text{News}\}$ indexes the three component indices and $\sigma_{j}^2$ represents the sample variance of each index. This weighting scheme assigns greater weight to indices with lower variance, under the assumption that lower variance reflects more reliable signals. The combined index thus integrates search-based retail investor attention, machine learning predictions, and professional media coverage into a single measure.

\subsection{Econometric Framework}

\subsubsection{Stationarity and Data Transformations}

We conduct Augmented Dickey-Fuller (ADF) tests for all variables to ensure appropriate transformations for time series analysis. CDS levels exhibit non-stationarity ($p = 0.09$), which could produce misleading results in regression analysis. Following \cite{hamilton1994time}, we transform CDS to log-returns:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cds_transform}
    \Delta \log(\text{CDS})_t = \log(\text{CDS}_t) - \log(\text{CDS}_{t-1})
\end{equation}
The FSI measures are bounded between 0 and 1 and exhibit stationarity ($p < 0.01$ for ADF tests). We standardize all variables to have zero mean and unit variance for comparability of effect sizes across specifications.

\subsubsection{Local Projections Impulse Response Functions}

To assess the predictive power of the FSI for sovereign credit risk, we employ Local Projections \citep{jorda2005estimation}. This method directly estimates the response of CDS spreads to FSI innovations at each forecast horizon, offering advantages over Vector Autoregression when the true data-generating process is uncertain. Local Projections are more robust to potential misspecification and handle persistent variables effectively.

For each horizon $h = 0, 1, \ldots, 12$ months, we estimate:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lp}
    \sum_{j=0}^{h} \Delta \log(\text{CDS})_{t+j} = \alpha_h + \beta_h \cdot \Delta \text{FSI}_t + \sum_{k=1}^{p} \gamma_{h,k} \cdot \Delta \log(\text{CDS})_{t-k} + \varepsilon_{t+h}
\end{equation}
where the dependent variable $\sum_{j=0}^{h} \Delta \log(\text{CDS})_{t+j}$ is the cumulative log-return of CDS spreads from $t$ to $t+h$. The coefficient $\beta_h$ represents the cumulative impulse response at horizon $h$, measuring the cumulative percentage change in CDS spreads following a one-standard-deviation FSI innovation. We include $p = 4$ lags of CDS log-returns as controls to account for persistence in sovereign risk dynamics. Newey-West heteroskedasticity and autocorrelation consistent (HAC) standard errors with bandwidth $h + 1$ address the overlapping observations inherent in the cumulative specification \citep{newey1987simple}.

\subsubsection{Granger Causality Tests}

We complement the IRF analysis with Granger causality tests \citep{granger1969investigating}, which examine whether past FSI values improve predictions of CDS movements beyond what past CDS values alone provide. This analysis helps establish the direction of predictive relationships and distinguish between scenarios where the FSI leads market outcomes versus merely reflecting contemporaneous conditions. We test at multiple lag lengths (1--4 months) and report the minimum $p$-value across specifications.

An important validation exercise concerns the correlation between FSI measures and realized market volatility. The Dictionary FSI demonstrates strong correlation with IBOVESPA volatility ($r = 0.70$, $p < 0.001$), supporting its validity as a stress measure. This positive correlation indicates that periods of elevated search activity for stress-related terms coincide with periods of heightened market volatility. In contrast, the ML FSI exhibits negative correlation with volatility ($r = -0.21$, $p = 0.002$), suggesting counterintuitive behavior where the LASSO-selected queries move inversely to actual market stress. The News FSI shows moderate positive correlation ($r = 0.33$, $p < 0.001$), falling between the dictionary and ML approaches.

Figure~\ref{fig:irf_main} presents our main finding: the cumulative impulse response of CDS spreads to a one-standard-deviation Dictionary FSI shock. The response is positive and statistically significant at all 13 horizons (months 0--12), indicating that increases in search-based stress reliably predict subsequent widening of sovereign credit spreads.

The cumulative effect reaches +81.45\% after 12 months, meaning a one-standard-deviation increase in the FSI predicts an 81.45\% cumulative increase in CDS spreads over the following year. This substantial magnitude reflects the FSI's ability to capture shifts in market sentiment that precede credit risk repricing. The persistence of the effect---remaining significant through all 13 months---indicates that the FSI captures fundamental shifts in risk perception rather than transitory noise.

The Dictionary FSI substantially outperforms alternatives, exhibiting the only consistently positive and significant impulse response pattern. This counterintuitive finding suggests that the LASSO-selected queries, while potentially predictive of short-term equity returns in the training sample, fail to capture stress dynamics relevant for sovereign credit risk. The Combined FSI shows positive but substantially weaker effects (+33.44\%), with only 3 significant horizons out of 13. Combining the strongly predictive Dictionary FSI with the inversely-behaving ML FSI dilutes rather than enhances the signal. This result highlights a limitation of mechanical combination approaches when component indices capture fundamentally different phenomena.

The Dictionary FSI exhibits marginally significant predictive power for CDS movements ($p = 0.065$), falling just outside conventional significance thresholds but consistent with the strong IRF results. More strikingly, the FSI strongly Granger-causes IBOVESPA volatility ($p < 0.0001$), indicating robust predictive content for equity market stress. This finding suggests that retail investor search behavior contains information about future market conditions beyond what is captured in current prices.

The result that Dictionary FSI Granger-causes News FSI ($p = 0.004$) is particularly interesting. This suggests that retail investor search behavior leads professional media coverage of financial stress, consistent with an information diffusion hypothesis where retail investors react to emerging stress signals before journalists compile and publish their analysis.

The bidirectional causality between volatility and the Dictionary FSI suggests feedback mechanisms: search-based stress both anticipates and responds to market movements. This pattern is consistent with theories of information diffusion in financial markets, where investor attention responds to price movements while simultaneously containing independent predictive information.

\subsection{Discussion}

The strong performance of the Dictionary FSI relative to ML and Combined approaches carries important methodological implications for the construction of text-based financial indicators. Expert-constructed query dictionaries, when carefully adapted to local language and institutional context, can outperform data-driven approaches that may overfit to idiosyncratic patterns in training data. The LASSO regularization in the ML approach optimizes for predicting equity returns, but this objective may not align with capturing broader financial stress relevant for credit markets.

The finding that the Combined FSI underperforms the Dictionary FSI alone highlights a more general point about index aggregation. When component indices capture fundamentally different phenomena---as evidenced by the negative correlation between Dictionary and ML FSI---simple combination methods can destroy rather than enhance predictive power. This suggests that researchers should carefully validate the coherence of component measures before combining them.

The predictive content of the Dictionary FSI for sovereign credit risk has practical implications for market participants and policymakers. The FSI provides a near-real-time indicator of stress perception that predicts CDS movements up to 12 months ahead. This lead time could be valuable for risk management and policy surveillance, though the magnitude of effects suggests the FSI captures gradual shifts in risk sentiment rather than sudden crises.

Finally, the result that retail search behavior leads professional media coverage suggests an interesting information hierarchy. Rather than retail investors simply reacting to news, search patterns may reflect direct observations of economic conditions or social transmission of concerns that journalists subsequently formalize in their reporting. This finding contributes to the broader literature on information production and aggregation in financial markets.
